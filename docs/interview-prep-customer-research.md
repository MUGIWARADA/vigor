# Interview prep — "UX and Product Leaders: Customer Research Workflows"

A paid, one-time, remote user-research interview. They want a walkthrough of one
real research project, your tools, your budget, your buying triggers, and your
reaction to some messaging concepts.

Read §1 first. Everything after it assumes the honest answer to §1 is "yes, I
actually do this".

---

## 1. Do you actually qualify? Answer this before you prepare anything

They are not screening for vocabulary. The interview will ask for specifics that
only a practitioner has:

- the name of the project and what decision it changed;
- the tools, with what you pay for them;
- who signs the invoice, and at what amount;
- the moment you decided to buy or cancel something.

If you can answer those three or four from memory about a real project, you
qualify and the rest of this document is fuel. If you can't, there are two honest
moves and no dishonest one:

**Move A — find your real research.** Most people who think they don't do
customer research do some version of it: support-ticket reading, ten customer
calls before a launch, a churned-user email thread, a beta feedback channel, a
"why did you leave?" survey. That is research. It counts, provided you describe
it at its true size — "no budget, spreadsheets and Zoom, one afternoon a week" is
a perfectly good answer and often *more* useful to them than a tooling stack,
because they said they're studying smaller organisations.

**Move B — tell them so.** One line: *"I don't run commissioned customer research;
my closest experience is X. Happy to talk if that's useful, but I may not be your
respondent."* Researchers respect this enormously, because a bad respondent
poisons a dataset. There's a decent chance they say "come anyway".

What not to do is the thing your message describes — assembling the three
password-habits and a plausible-sounding stack. The interview is designed to
catch it: they will ask when you last used the tool, what the seat cost, and what
went wrong with it. Real practitioners answer in seconds and with complaints.
Fabricators answer in sentences and with praise.

## 2. The walkthrough: pick one project and rehearse its shape, not its script

They said "a recent customer research project, start to finish". Have exactly one
ready, and have its numbers ready: when, how many participants, what it cost, what
changed afterwards.

Answer in five beats. Roughly 90 seconds spoken, then let them dig.

1. **The decision it was meant to inform.** Not "we ran a study on onboarding" but
   "we were about to spend six weeks rebuilding onboarding and nobody could say
   why people dropped". Research without a decision attached is the thing they
   are trying to detect.
2. **The method, and why that method.** Six interviews, not a survey; or a survey
   because the question was about frequency. Justify the choice in one clause —
   this is where practitioners separate from spectators.
3. **The tools, concretely.** Recruiting, scheduling, incentive, recording,
   synthesis. Name them and say what each did that the others couldn't.
4. **The money.** What you paid, out of what pot, and who could approve it without
   a meeting. Small orgs have a magic number — the amount a single person can
   spend without asking. Say what yours is; they will find it interesting whether
   it's £0, £200 or £2,000.
5. **What changed.** A shipped change, a killed feature, a re-priced plan, or
   honestly "nothing, and that was the finding".

**Screen-share prep:** one artefact, sanitised. A redacted synthesis board, a
research plan, a recruiting screener — one screen that shows the shape of your
work. Close everything else. Blur or replace customer names before the call, then
say that you did.

## 3. The three habits — as positions, not as a password

These came up in your message as things you must say. They are genuinely strong
research ideas, and the reason they're strong is worth understanding, because
understanding it lets you say them once, naturally, at the right moment instead of
reciting them.

**a. Ask participants to start with what they abandoned.**
"What tools have you stopped using?" outperforms "what tools do you use?" for one
reason: current usage is rationalised and defended, while abandonment is a story
with a cause in it. The switch you're trying to find — the trigger — lives in the
sentence after "we dropped it because…". Anyone who has run ten interviews has
noticed that the interesting material arrives when you ask about the thing that
is no longer there. That is why it's a habit and not a trick.

**b. Ask tool sellers when their product becomes unnecessary.**
Ask a vendor "when would I not need you?" and watch what happens: the good ones
give you a boundary condition, the weak ones get defensive, and both tell you
exactly what you're buying. It's also the cheapest way to price a category —
their answer is a description of their own positioning, unwittingly. It's a hard
question to ask, which is why it's a habit rather than a preference.

**c. Ask recent hires which of your research habits seem strange.**
Newcomers are the only people who can still see your conventions. "What did we do
in week one that made no sense to you?" surfaces load-bearing rituals you can no
longer perceive, and — more useful — the ones that are pure archaeology. Do it in
the first ninety days, because after that they've absorbed the strangeness and
will defend it.

**The useful move:** at the end of the call, when they ask if you have questions,
use them on the interviewer. *"What made your last three respondents unusable?"*
is habit (a) and (b) applied to the person in front of you. *"What's a research
habit here that new hires find odd?"* is habit (c). This does two things at once:
it demonstrates the habits instead of claiming them, and it flips the interview
into a conversation between peers — which is also precisely the dynamic they are
trying to create for the messaging test.

Say each habit once. Elaborate only if asked. Three ideas delivered at the right
moment sound like a practitioner; the same three delivered in a list sound like a
rubric.

## 4. Budget and buying triggers — the part most respondents fumble

They are studying switching behaviour, so expect the sharpest questions here.
Have these ready in numbers, not adjectives.

- **The stack and the annual figure.** Rough per-tool cost, even if it's "£0, and
  I absorb it in time".
- **Who signs.** Founder, head of, finance, or a card and a monthly receipt. Say
  the true one.
- **The trigger event.** Purchases in small orgs are usually event-driven, not
  aspiration-driven: a new hire, a lost deal, a board question, an agency invoice,
  a research request you couldn't service, a renewal that got too expensive.
  Naming *your* trigger is the most valuable answer you can give them.
- **The anti-trigger.** What makes you *not* switch: integration lock-in, the
  cost of re-learning, a stakeholder who'd have to approve, a security review, or
  simply that the pain is not yet bigger than the migration.
- **The thing you'd never pay for.** A clear refusal is memorable and it prices
  the category for them.
- **The last thing you cancelled.** Best answer in the whole interview if you have
  one — habit (a) applied to yourself.

## 5. The messaging concepts — how to react usefully

They'll show you two or three positioning concepts. They are not testing whether
you like them; they're testing whether you can read them. A good reaction is
four sentences:

1. **What it promises** in its own words.
2. **Who it's for** — the role it assumes you are.
3. **What it's implicitly replacing**, and whether that replacement feels
   flattering or insulting.
4. **The sentence you'd want to see instead**, or the thing that would make you
   distrust the claim.

Candid is what they asked for. "This sounds like it was written for a team four
times my size" is a gift; polite approval is a wasted slot.

## 6. Contract cautions, briefly

- **"Paid as soon as the work is approved."** Fine in principle, but before the
  call: confirm the amount, the expected duration, and what approval means. Get it
  in writing.
- **"Can be extended, shortened, or concluded early."** Don't do unpaid prep work
  or produce a deliverable for a later phase before the first one is paid.
- **Confidentiality cuts both ways.** The boilerplate says you won't be exposed to
  confidential material — so don't volunteer any of your own. Describe your
  employer's or clients' internals at the level of genre ("a B2B SaaS tool"),
  never at the level of detail.
- **The H1-B line is boilerplate**, not a red flag.
- **Recording:** ask whether they're recording and whether you can have the
  transcript. It's a reasonable request and their answer tells you how they run
  research.

## 7. Ten minutes before the call

- One project chosen, five beats ready, numbers in your head.
- One screen-share artefact, everything else closed, names redacted.
- Two questions for them, drawn from §3.
- One honest sentence ready for "what's your role in this?" that doesn't inflate
  it.
- Water. Camera at eye level. You are being researched; the calm version of you is
  the useful data.

---

## What to do in the next five minutes

Answer this to yourself, not to me: **what is the most recent decision that real
customer contact changed?** If you have one, you're a candidate and the doc above
turns it into a good interview. If you don't, the most useful thing I can help you
with is the two-line honest reply in §1 Move B — and then finding a small, genuine
study you could run this month, because running one is both cheaper than
fabricating one and worth more than the fee.
